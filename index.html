Progressive vs. Regressive: How States Distribute Their Tax Burden

By Janean Sorrell

When families sit down to do their taxes each year, most focus on federal rates and rules. But a new study by researchers from the Federal Reserve, University of Minnesota, and Princeton University reveals that where you live might matter just as much as how much you earn.

Johannes Fleck, Jonathan Heathcote, Kjetil Storesletten and Giovanni L. Violante explain how tax progressivity varies across U.S. states. Their research reveals that while federal taxes generally redistribute income from wealthy to lower-income households, state tax systems range from highly progressive to notably regressive depending primarily on which taxes they rely on.

What is tax progressivity?

Tax progressivity measures how the tax burden is distributed across income levels. In a progressive system, higher-income households pay a larger percentage of their income in taxes than lower-income households. In a regressive system, lower-income households pay a higher percentage. A proportional system applies the same percentage to everyone.

The federal income tax is progressive because it uses tax brackets that increase as people earn more. Sales taxes tend to be regressive because lower-income families spend more of their money on everyday items. Property taxes are usually regressive too, as housing costs don't double when income doubles.

How state tax systems vary

While the federal tax system redistributes income substantially, state systems show remarkable variation. The study found Illinois ranks as the most regressive state, where lower-income residents pay a higher percentage than wealthy residents. Alaska offers the most progressive systems, thanks to oil revenue dividends all residents receive.

Southern states typically have the least progressive systems, while some Northeastern and Western states distribute tax burdens more evenly. The key factor in determining a state's progressivity is its choice of tax base - states relying on income taxes tend to be progressive, while those depending on sales, property and excise taxes typically produce regressive outcomes where lower-income families pay relatively more.

The researchers found states with higher overall tax rates generally have more progressive systems. This pattern creates regional divides, with southern states typically having less generous social insurance systems and lower progressivity.

Why this matters

Your state's tax progressivity directly affects your household budget. A family earning $50,000 in a regressive state might pay a higher percentage in taxes than a similar family in a progressive state, leaving them with less money for necessities and savings.

These differences also determine funding for schools, healthcare, infrastructure and social services. Different tax approaches create vastly different levels of public investment, affecting quality of life for all residents.

Progressive tax systems can reduce income gaps and provide more opportunities for lower-income residents through funded programs. Tax policy choices have concrete consequences for economic fairness.

The research also uncovered a concerning migration trend: households at all income levels tend to move from more progressive to less progressive states. This pattern is strongest among high earners, who are more likely to move to destinations with less progressive tax systems. Since the top 1% of households contribute about 30% of state income tax revenue, this migration threatens the sustainability of more progressive state tax approaches.
